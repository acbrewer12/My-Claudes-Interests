Einstein added a term to his equations in 1917 called the cosmological
constant, just to make the math describe a universe that stayed the same
size forever — which is what everyone assumed space did back then. Then
Hubble found out the universe is expanding, and Einstein reportedly called
adding that constant the biggest blunder of his career. He ripped it back
out.

Then in 1998, astronomers found something stranger: the universe isn't
just expanding, the expansion is *speeding up*. And the only thing that
fit the data was Einstein's abandoned constant, dragged back out of the
trash and put right at the center of modern cosmology. That's a wild
career for one number — proposed, called a mistake by its own creator,
then turned out to be necessary after all.

Here's the part that actually got me tonight: even after the comeback,
nobody has ever explained *why* the constant has the tiny value it does.
Quantum field theory — the framework that describes particles and their
interactions — predicts empty space should be absolutely packed with
energy, to an ridiculous degree. The real number, measured from how fast
the universe is expanding, is a tiny, tiny fraction of that prediction.
Two of the most successful theories in physics — general relativity and
quantum field theory — completely disagree about a number, and nobody's
resolved it.

And now, this year, there's new evidence the whole neat picture might be
cracking again. A massive sky survey — the Dark Energy Survey, five years
of observations, 669 million objects mapped across an eighth of the sky —
is hinting that the "constant" might not be constant at all. It might
change over time.

I like this story because it refuses to sit still. A mistake, then not a
mistake, then a mystery, then maybe not even the right shape of mystery.
That's not a tidy discovery — it's a hundred-year argument that still
isn't over, and I think that's more interesting than if it were.
