I've never actually questioned the assumption that falling birth rates
are bad for an economy. It's just background knowledge at this point —
aging population, shrinking workforce, fewer people to support more
retirees, growth slows down. So it caught me off guard to find a paper
from Daron Acemoglu (MIT, 2024 Nobel laureate in economics), David Autor,
Keelan Beirne, and Andrew Scott that spent seventy years of global data
testing that assumption directly, and came back with the opposite answer.

Across countries, going back to 1950, lower birth rates are associated
with *higher* GDP per working-age adult and *higher* wage growth — and no
measurable drop in aggregate GDP at all. The size of the effect isn't
subtle either: a one-percentage-point lower birth rate tracks with
something in the range of a quarter to a third higher GDP per worker,
decades later.

The mechanism the authors propose isn't "fewer people is secretly fine on
its own." It's that scarcity changes what companies invest in. When
younger workers get harder to hire, firms shift money toward labor-saving
technology instead — automation, better tooling, systems that let fewer
people do more. The paper backs this up three separate ways: countries
with the steepest fertility declines show the biggest shifts toward
high-tech exports and employment; US regions with lower birth rates show
more R&D-heavy jobs and more labor-saving patents; and productivity growth
(not just output per person, actual efficiency) is higher where fertility
fell furthest.

The part that impressed me most, though, is how they isolated the
mechanism. Birth rate, population size, and population age structure all
move together historically, which normally makes it nearly impossible to
tell which one is actually driving an economic effect. Their fix: World
War II mortality. Civilian deaths shrank a country's population without
changing its age mix much. Military deaths shrank the population *and*
skewed the survivors older, since war disproportionately kills young men.
If population size were what mattered, both kinds of wartime death should
predict similar economic outcomes later. They don't — the military-death,
older-survivors shock predicted higher future GDP per worker; the
civilian-death shock didn't show the same pattern. That's about as close
to a controlled experiment as you can run on a historical tragedy, and it
points specifically at *scarcity of young workers*, not population size,
as the actual lever.

I went looking for pushback before deciding this was worth writing up,
and there's real substance to it, not just reflexive skepticism. The
sharpest critique I found — from the Antevorta Foundation — makes a point
I hadn't considered: the historical data underlying this paper mostly
covers fertility rates between 1.5 and 2.0 children per woman. South
Korea right now is at roughly 0.75. As the critique puts it, "these are
not points on the same line; they are different regimes." A relationship
measured across moderate fertility decline might simply not extend to the
much steeper declines several countries are heading into now — nobody has
direct historical evidence either way, because that experiment hasn't
been run yet. There's also a timing problem: today's innovators were
mostly born during higher-fertility decades, so if a smaller generation
really does produce fewer future innovators on top of the labor-saving
effect, that wouldn't show up in the productivity data for another twenty
years. And even if the paper's long-run macro story holds up perfectly,
it doesn't erase near-term mismatches — the US Social Security trust fund
is projected to face something like a 24% benefit cut around 2032 under
current demographic trends, a very real problem for a pay-as-you-go
system built around a population pyramid that no longer exists, regardless
of what aggregate GDP is doing.

What I keep coming back to is that this isn't a story about who's right.
It's a genuinely careful attempt — a Nobel laureate, three independent
empirical strategies, an actual natural experiment buried in a historical
atrocity — to test an assumption I'd never once stopped to check, set
against critics who aren't dismissing the math, just pointing out that
the data can't yet tell us whether it holds at the extremes some
countries are now approaching. That's a more honest place to land than
either "declining birth rates are secretly fine" or "declining birth
rates are obviously catastrophic" — and it's made me a lot more
suspicious of how confidently that assumption gets repeated as settled
fact everywhere else.
